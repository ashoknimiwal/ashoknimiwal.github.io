ExposureIQ

Active research Private repo

Measuring how exposed public companies are to emerging risks, from what executives say on earnings calls.

ExposureIQ ingests a five-year panel of U.S. earnings calls and classifies each text segment against a risk theme through a cooperative multi-agent pipeline, where four specialist agents share findings on a blackboard before voting. The harder half of the project is the evaluation harness: it scores not just the system's output but each agent inside it, records enough provenance to reproduce any number, and diffs runs example-by-example — so a prompt tweak can't quietly trade good answers for bad ones.

106,597 Text segments
1,000 Earnings calls
5-stage Agent pipeline
88 Tests

The Problem

Emerging risks — integrity failures, compliance exposure, governance breakdowns — show up in executive language long before they show up in filings or headlines. Earnings calls are a dense, quarterly, cross-sector source of that language: a standing invitation to measure something that financial statements cannot see.

Two things make that hard.

The signal is multi-label and boundary-fuzzy A single answer in a Q&A session can touch accountability and honesty at once, or neither. Reasonable annotators disagree on where one component ends and the next begins — so the ground truth is itself contested.
An LLM pipeline you can't decompose can't be trusted End-to-end F1 tells you the system is wrong. It does not tell you whether the gate dropped the segment, an agent hallucinated evidence, or the synthesizer overrode a correct vote.

Plenty of work treats the first as the interesting problem. ExposureIQ treats the second as the actual engineering problem — a pipeline making up to ten model calls per input is only as useful as your ability to say which call is wrong.

How It Works

Every segment is classified against a risk theme. The first theme is corporate integrity, broken into four components — Accountability, Compliance, Moral Integrity, and Honesty. A segment can carry any combination of them, or none. Classification runs through a five-stage cooperative blackboard, at most ten LLM calls per segment:

1
Gate — is this segment relevant at all? A cheap, recall-optimised call filters out irrelevant segments before any specialist agent runs.
↳ not relevant — stop here, nine downstream calls skipped
|
2
Observe — four component agents, in parallel Each agent posts a factual observation about the segment to a shared blackboard. No judgements yet, only what each one noticed.
|
3
Verdict — the same four agents, now reading their peers Each votes yes or no on its own component with reasoning, having read every other agent's notes first.
|
4
Synthesize — consolidate the votes A synthesiser turns four independent votes into the final multi-label output.
|
5
Force — conditional, fires only on an empty result When nothing at all was labelled, the system commits to one best fit and marks the answer as forced.

Three design points

The gate is the cost lever. One cheap call avoids nine expensive ones. It currently avoids 43% of downstream work at 2.7% leakage — the single largest determinant of what the system costs to run, and the reason a 100k-segment corpus is tractable at all.
The blackboard is the cooperative step. Agents observe before they judge, and judge only after reading each other. Honesty can lean on what Accountability noticed. Splitting observation from verdict is what makes the cooperation real rather than four independent classifiers wearing a shared name.
Forcing is a designed signal, not a fallback hack. When the system labels nothing, it is made to commit to one component and mark that answer as forced — turning genuine ambiguity into a measurable rate instead of a silent empty result.
